# Chapter I: INTRODUCTION

## Background of the Study

A standard fixed-camera illegal parking detection system, when tested in real-world urban traffic, cannot mechanically distinguish a vehicle waiting at an unseen red light from a vehicle that has been illegally parked for an hour. Both produce identical spatial-temporal signatures: they are stationary inside a restricted zone with an expired timer. The root cause of this failure mode is architectural: standard violation decision logic evaluates the candidate vehicle in complete isolation from the dynamic traffic system in which it is embedded.

At the foundational level, automated Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS) rely on computer vision architectures to extract spatial data from fixed traffic cameras. The vehicle detection stage utilizes advanced single-stage object detectors such as YOLO to facilitate rapid object recognition [38]. To maintain persistent vehicle identities across consecutive frames, frameworks integrate multi-object tracking algorithms [42], [50]. High-speed tracking-by-detection methods, such as Intersection-over-Union (IoU) trackers, have proven highly effective for maintaining track continuity with minimal computational overhead under fixed-camera conditions [54]. 

While vehicle detection and tracking stages are highly robust, the subsequent violation decision logic remains structurally flawed. The conventional method tracks whether a vehicle enters a predefined polygonal Region of Interest (ROI) and increments a frame counter, frequently utilizing a standard temporal threshold of 60 seconds [18], [45]. If the elapsed time exceeds this threshold, the system mechanically flags the vehicle as an illegal parking candidate. 

However, evaluating vehicles independently introduces a fundamental vulnerability grounded in traffic flow theory. According to kinematic wave theory, vehicles in an urban corridor are causally coupled; they stop collectively due to upstream conditions, such as traffic signal phase changes or propagating congestion shockwaves [53]. A vehicle stopped at a red light is causally coupled to surrounding vehicles by a shared external signal. In contrast, genuine illegal parking is an individual-level anomaly with no causal coupling to surrounding traffic flow. Therefore, a vehicle whose stationary state is temporally decorrelated from the surrounding traffic's collective motion state is theoretically more likely to be a genuine violation. Because standard algorithms ignore this theoretical distinction and evaluate candidates in strict isolation, they inherently misclassify legitimate, causally coupled traffic stops as illegal parking [9], [15], [16], [31].

## Gap/Opportunity

To mitigate traffic-induced false positives, current fixed-camera literature attempts to rely on fixed physical infrastructure or static environmental markers visible in the camera frame. For example, frameworks define pedestrian detection zones on crosswalks as proxies for inferring red-light phases [9], or rely on detecting specific no-parking signboards as event triggers [37]. These static infrastructure dependencies present a significant structural limitation: they fail under physical occlusion or suboptimal camera angles [9], [22], [37]. 

An alternative state-of-the-art approach eschews temporal tracking entirely in favor of semantic spatial co-occurrence, defining a violation as the simultaneous detection of a vehicle, a human, and a no-parking sign within a single frame [1]. However, this purely spatial paradigm suffers from severe empirical limitations. While the methodology reported a high precision of 0.9841 for a littering class, precision for the illegal parking class dropped significantly to 0.7467, yielding 509 combined false positives out of 2,803 total detections [1]. By mathematical deduction, the vast majority of these 509 false positives belong to the parking class. This empirical failure demonstrates that without temporal continuity and contextual awareness of surrounding traffic, single-frame semantic models inevitably mischaracterize legitimate traffic flow interruptions as violations.

In contrast to fixed-camera methodologies, researchers utilizing mobile camera platforms (dashcams and crowdsensing smartphones) explicitly analyze the collective behavior and spatial relationships of surrounding vehicles to deduce the ambient traffic state [15], [16], [33]. However, these mobile methodologies analyze surrounding traffic primarily to compensate for ego-motion uncertainty, relying heavily on hardcoded mathematical distance thresholds rather than machine-learned decision boundaries.

A critical gap exists in fixed-camera ITS environments. Fixed-camera systems possess perfectly stable ground-plane coordinates, which can be precisely calibrated using 2D homography [49], and generate surrounding kinematic data continuously as a natural byproduct of multi-object tracking [54]. Yet, current frameworks discard this contextual data entirely after the tracking phase. No existing fixed-camera framework utilizes the aggregate, dynamic kinematic state of surrounding tracked vehicles as a machine-learned decision input to differentiate genuine violations from legitimate traffic stops. 

## Statement of the Problem

Based on the limitations identified in existing computer vision-based illegal parking detection frameworks, this study seeks to answer the following research questions:

- How effectively can the aggregate spatiotemporal motion data of surrounding tracked vehicles, specifically modeled through a 6-dimensional feature vector ($R, C, \bar{d}, \sigma_d, \Delta R, d_{\text{cand}}$), serve as a decision input for a trained classifier to differentiate genuine illegal parking from legitimate collective traffic stops, without relying on fixed external infrastructure?
- How does the proposed context-aware spatiotemporal classifier perform compared to a state-of-the-art, single-frame spatial co-occurrence baseline [1] in terms of precision, recall, F1-score, and false positive reduction under real-world traffic conditions?

## Objectives

The objectives of the study are as follows:

- To develop a computer vision pipeline that integrates an XGBoost classifier, trained on a 6-dimensional spatiotemporal feature vector, to differentiate genuine illegal parking violations from legitimate collective traffic stops without reliance on fixed external infrastructure.
- To evaluate and compare the performance of the proposed context-aware classifier against a single-frame spatial co-occurrence baseline [1], specifically measuring precision, recall, F1-score, and the reduction of traffic-induced false positives.

## Significance of the Study

This study is significant for law enforcement agencies and local government units, because automated monitoring frameworks reduce the need for continuous manual surveillance of restricted zones [3], [18]. The primary objective of these automated deployments is to provide highly reliable, objective identification of genuine infractions [19], [43]. However, because standard systems frequently generate false positive alerts during traffic congestion, human operators are forced to expend time manually reviewing and dismissing invalid alerts. By validating a context-aware classifier that mechanically filters these legitimate stops, this study minimizes administrative overhead, establishing a more robust technical baseline that allows enforcement personnel to allocate their resources efficiently [19].

For urban planners and policymakers, the study offers a practical solution that conforms to the constraints of edge-AI enabled video analytics [5]. It does not require expensive supplementary sensors, heavy end-to-end deep neural networks, or traffic signal API feeds. The framework is designed to operate on fixed cameras already installed in urban areas, making it a highly cost-effective option for managing curb space [9], [19]. It supports the goals of SDG 9 and SDG 11 by assisting in the creation of organized urban environments, while aligning with the Philippine Development Plan 2023-2028 and AmBisyon Natin 2040 [6].

The general public benefits from the reduction of illegally parked vehicles, which block roads and emergency routes, increasing the probability of rear-end collisions [37]. A system that effectively enforces parking restrictions reduces cruising time, which consequently lowers fuel consumption and urban emissions [6], [28].

Finally, this study contributes to the academic community by directly addressing a documented structural limitation in vision-based violation decision logic. The literature demonstrates that evaluating candidate vehicles in strict isolation inherently produces false positive detections during collective traffic stops. By applying traffic flow theory to computer vision and validating a spatiotemporal classification framework that utilizes the kinematic state of surrounding traffic, this research elevates the theoretical and technical foundation for complex behavior analysis in fixed-camera environments.

## Scope and Limitations of the Study

This study focuses solely on fixed-camera, computer vision-based frameworks for detecting illegal parking. It does not include mobile camera platforms, dashcams, UAVs, or crowdsensing systems [16], [20], [33]. The architecture utilizes video footage from a single camera and does not integrate external hardware, additional sensors, or traffic signal API feeds.

The contribution of this study is strictly restricted to the violation decision logic phase. The vision pipeline utilizes pretrained YOLOv8 weights [42] and a standard IoU-based multi-object tracker [8], [54]. The deployment constraint of real-time edge processing [5] dictates that the violation logic must incur minimal computational overhead; therefore, an XGBoost classifier is utilized over computationally heavy end-to-end deep neural networks. The study does not propose new object detection architectures or resolve upstream tracking identity switches.

The empirical evaluation of this study is geographically and structurally bounded to a single elevated fixed-camera vantage point overlooking an unsignalized urban roadway corridor [test site location to be specified upon site confirmation]. The research team collects a primary video dataset at the selected location, selecting a site with urban traffic density and road geometry comparable to typical on-street surveillance environments [1]. The restricted zone (Region of Interest) is defined as a static closed polygon spatially grounded in the statutory provisions of Republic Act No. 4136 (Land Transportation and Traffic Code of the Philippines). The framework does not claim universal, zero-shot deployment across arbitrary uncalibrated locations; camera intrinsic parameters and 2D ground-plane homography calibration [49] are specific to the physical camera mounting height, tilt angle, and site geometry of the designated test location.

Data acquisition is strictly confined to daytime peak traffic hours (16:00 to 18:00) under clear or overcast weather conditions to ensure adequate surrounding vehicle density for contextual feature extraction. Adverse atmospheric conditions, such as severe precipitation, and low-illumination nighttime scenarios are explicitly excluded from the scope, as visual noise and illumination degradation compromise the tracking stability of lightweight bounding box detectors [36], [42].

Evaluation is limited to candidate vehicles that have remained stationary inside the restricted zone for a minimum temporal threshold of 60 seconds, consistent with standard ITS literature [18], [45]. Consequently, stationary events lasting less than 60 seconds are structurally excluded from the evaluation. 

The context-aware classifier explicitly requires the presence of at least two tracked vehicles in the scene to compute a mathematically valid contextual feature vector. If a candidate vehicle is completely isolated with no surrounding traffic, the system bypasses the classifier and gracefully degrades to standard temporal threshold logic. This fallback is highly optimal: in isolated scenarios characterized by extremely low traffic volume, standard timer-only logic performs perfectly because there are no collective traffic stops to misclassify. The context-aware classifier specifically targets the high-density urban scenarios where standard systems fail most severely.

The baseline method [1] is evaluated via a same-dataset reimplementation under identical test conditions. Because the proposed method defines a violation based on continuous dwell-time, whereas the baseline method defines a violation based on single-frame spatial co-occurrence, a structural distinction in the ground truth formulation exists. The comparative evaluation strictly tests discrimination ability within the pool of extended-duration stationary events. Validating the classifier's generalization capabilities across varying intersection geometries and highly divergent traffic patterns is identified as an area for future research.
